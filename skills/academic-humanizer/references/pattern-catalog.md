# Academic AI-pattern catalog

Use these patterns to diagnose generic or mechanical prose. They are editorial signals, not proof of authorship. Flag repeated cues or clusters, not isolated words.

## Decision rule

A revision is usually justified when either condition holds:

1. the same pattern recurs and makes the passage less specific; or
2. cues from at least two categories occur together and flatten the author's argument.

Do not rewrite a phrase merely because it appears in this catalog. Keep it when it names a real relation, follows a disciplinary convention, or is the clearest available wording.

## English-language patterns

### Generic framing

Watch for openings that could introduce almost any paper:

- “In recent years, X has attracted increasing attention”;
- “With the rapid development of X”;
- “Despite remarkable progress” without identifying the progress;
- “X plays an important role” without saying where or how.

Prefer the concrete problem, boundary, contradiction, or unknown already present in the source.

### Inflated significance and novelty

Watch for:

- pivotal, groundbreaking, transformative, revolutionary, unprecedented;
- “paves the way”, “opens new avenues”, “sheds light on”;
- repeated “novel”, “for the first time”, or “to the best of our knowledge”;
- universal conclusions drawn from bounded data.

Replace hype with the specific problem addressed, result obtained, or scope supported. Do not delete a defensible novelty claim when the author has evidence for it.

### Empty intensity and scale

Watch for “extensive”, “comprehensive”, “numerous”, “various”, “remarkable”, “robust”, or “significant” without visible scope, metric, or test.

Use supplied counts, datasets, conditions, effect sizes, or statistical evidence. If none is supplied, flag the gap instead of inventing specificity.

### Abstract or ornamental language

Watch for strings of abstract nouns, decorative metaphors, or fashionable verbs that do not add technical meaning, including filler uses of “leverage”, “underscore”, “showcase”, “delve”, “landscape”, “tapestry”, “foster”, and “facilitate”.

Prefer the real actor and action. Retain a listed word when it is technically exact.

### Mechanical structure

Watch for:

- repeated “Moreover”, “Furthermore”, “Additionally”, and “Importantly”;
- padded triples that exist only for rhythm;
- repeated “not only X but also Y” or “rather than merely X, Y” contrasts;
- contribution lists that say only “a novel method, extensive experiments, strong results”;
- headings or lead-ins that impose symmetry without matching the argument.

Let the logical relation carry the transition. Contribution lists should name concrete methods, evidence, artifacts, or findings already supported by the source.

### Clause stacking and synthetic cadence

Watch for sentences with several nested clauses, repeated participial tails, or a uniform sequence of similarly sized sentences. Typical weak endings include “..., highlighting X”, “..., underscoring Y”, and “..., thereby demonstrating Z” when the tail merely repeats the sentence.

Split or cut only when qualification and citation scope remain intact. Sentence-length variation is a diagnostic, not a target.

### Vague evidence and attribution

Watch for:

- “researchers believe”, “studies show”, or “it is widely accepted” without a supplied citation;
- citation dumping without explaining which source supports which proposition;
- “better”, “more robust”, or “substantial” without comparator and metric;
- “demonstrates” or “proves” where the evidence is observational or bounded.

Apply the claim-evidence contract. Never invent the missing source.

### Section mismatch

- Abstract: background overwhelms objective, result, or calibrated implication.
- Introduction: generic importance delays the actual gap.
- Methods: stylistic variety replaces stable procedural terminology.
- Results: interpretation or promotional language precedes the observation.
- Discussion: implications outrun findings or limitations disappear.
- Conclusion: the final sentence expands from a bounded sample to a universal claim.

## Chinese-language patterns

### Generic openings

Watch for:

- “随着……的快速发展”；
- “近年来，……日益受到广泛关注”；
- “在……背景下”；
- “……在……中发挥着重要作用”，但没有说明具体作用。

优先写清研究对象、现实矛盾、已有证据或尚未解决的问题。

### 空泛强调和意义拔高

Watch for:

- “具有重要的理论意义和现实意义”；
- “具有十分重要的价值”；
- “填补了研究空白”；
- “开辟了新的路径”；
- “深刻揭示”“充分证明”“有力说明”；
- “赋能”“助力”“推动高质量发展”等没有具体机制的表达。

把意义落到来源文本已有的对象、机制、结果或适用范围。不得凭空增加政策意义、实践价值或理论贡献。

### 机械衔接与排比

Watch for:

- 连续使用“首先、其次、再次、最后”；
- “一方面……另一方面……”并未形成真实对照；
- “不仅……而且……”重复出现；
- “值得注意的是”“不难发现”“毋庸置疑”“由此可见”；
- 为追求三点式结构而补出第三项。

只有在逻辑关系真实存在时保留连接词。不要为了节奏改变论证结构。

### 名词堆叠和抽象动词

Watch for long strings such as “实现……的提升与促进”, “开展……的构建工作”, or repeated “进行、实现、促进、推动” that hide the actor and action.

Use a concrete subject and verb already supported by the source. Preserve conventional technical nominalizations when they are standard in the field.

### 均匀句式与总结尾巴

Watch for paragraphs in which every sentence has the same rhythm, every claim receives a parallel summary, or sentences repeatedly end with “从而为……提供参考/支撑/借鉴”.

Remove conclusions that merely restate the previous clause. Keep a conclusion when it adds a supported implication or necessary boundary.

### 证据模糊

Watch for “研究表明”“大量研究发现”“普遍认为” without citations supplied by the author, and for causal verbs applied to correlational designs.

Request evidence, preserve the weaker original meaning, or flag the issue. Never fabricate a citation or convert association into causation.

## False-positive protections

Do not automatically remove:

- passive voice when the actor is irrelevant;
- “we” in fields where first-person plural is standard;
- calibrated hedges such as “may”, “suggests”, “可能”, and “提示”;
- repeated technical terms required for precision;
- conventional section language that performs a real rhetorical function;
- an occasional em dash, semicolon, parallel structure, or three-item list;
- terminology that happens to overlap with a popular AI-word list.

The target is information density and author-specific scholarly voice, not stylistic eccentricity.
